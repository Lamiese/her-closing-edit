If you’ve seen people talking about “high-ticket closing” online, you may have come away with more questions than answers. This guide explains what the role actually is, in plain English, so you can decide whether it’s worth exploring further.

## What is high-ticket closing?

High-ticket closing is the part of a sales process where a salesperson, known as a closer, holds a conversation with someone who is considering buying a higher-priced product or service. The aim of that conversation is to help the person make a clear, informed decision.

There’s no official price at which something becomes “high-ticket”. In practice, the term is used for offers that are expensive enough that most people want to speak to a real person before they commit. That’s exactly why the closer’s role exists.

## What does a closer actually do?

A closer’s day mostly revolves around scheduled calls with prospective customers, often called prospects or leads. On those calls, a closer will usually:

* Learn about the person’s situation, goals and challenges
* Understand what has stopped them solving the problem so far
* Explain how the offer works and whether it fits what they need
* Answer questions and talk through concerns honestly
* Discuss the investment and payment options
* Ask for a decision, and handle the next steps if the answer is yes

Around the calls, there’s usually preparation, note-taking in a CRM (customer relationship management system), follow-up messages and regular training or call reviews with a sales manager.

A good closer isn’t a pushy salesperson. The best ones are closer to advisers: they listen far more than they talk, and they’re comfortable telling someone when an offer isn’t right for them.

## What kinds of products and services are involved?

High-ticket closing is common wherever an offer is valuable, considered and usually sold to one person at a time. Typical examples include:

* Coaching and mentoring programmes
* Online courses with personal support
* Consultancy and business services
* Professional training and certifications
* Premium memberships and done-for-you services

You’ll find closers working for individual coaches, online education businesses and larger companies with full sales teams.

## What does a typical sales call involve?

Every company has its own process, but many calls follow a similar shape:

1. **Opening.** Building rapport and setting out how the call will run, so the prospect knows what to expect.
2. **Discovery.** Asking thoughtful questions to understand where the person is now, where they want to be and what’s in the way. This is usually the longest part of the call.
3. **Presenting the offer.** Explaining how the product or service could help, linked directly to what the prospect has shared.
4. **Discussing the investment.** Stating the price clearly and explaining any payment options.
5. **Addressing concerns.** Talking through questions or hesitations openly, rather than pressuring.
6. **The decision.** Asking whether they’d like to go ahead, and agreeing clear next steps either way.

Calls are often booked in advance after the prospect has filled in an application form or spoken briefly to an appointment setter. You can read more about that role in our guide to [closers vs appointment setters](/high-ticket-closer-vs-appointment-setter/).

## Remote vs in-person sales

Traditional sales roles often involve face-to-face meetings, showrooms or travel. Much of high-ticket closing now happens remotely, over video or phone calls, which is why it appeals to people who want location flexibility.

Remote closing still has structure. You’ll usually be expected to be available at set times, attend team calls and follow your company’s process. Calls may also be booked across different time zones, so it’s worth thinking about which hours you could realistically work.

## Common misconceptions

**“It’s quick, easy money.”** Closing is a skill that takes practice. Income can vary a great deal from month to month, especially at the start.

**“You need to be pushy.”** Pressure-based selling tends to lead to unhappy customers and refunds. Many companies actively look for consultative, empathetic closers.

**“You need years of sales experience.”** Some companies do hire beginners, particularly people with strong communication skills. You’ll still need to learn the foundations and prove yourself in interviews and role-plays.

**“Paying for a course guarantees a job.”** No legitimate training can guarantee you a role or an income. Be cautious of anyone who promises either.

## How do commissions work?

Many closing roles are paid partly or wholly in commission, which means you earn a percentage of the sales you close. Some roles also include a basic salary or a fixed fee, and others are commission only.

The commission percentage, when it’s paid and what happens if a customer later asks for a refund are all set by your agreement with the company, and they vary widely. ## Beginner FAQs

**Do I need a degree or qualification?**
Not usually. Employers tend to focus on communication skills, coachability and how you perform in a role-play.

**Is high-ticket closing the same as cold calling?**
Generally not. Closers usually speak to people who have already shown interest and booked a call, rather than contacting strangers.

**Can I do it part-time?**
Some roles are part-time or flexible, but many expect you to cover specific call slots. Check the expected hours before you apply.

**Is it legitimate?**
Closing itself is a normal sales role. As with any industry, some opportunities are better run than others. Our guide to [finding your first closing role](/how-to-find-high-ticket-closing-jobs/) covers how to spot warning signs.
