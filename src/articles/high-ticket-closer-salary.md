It’s one of the first questions people ask, and one of the hardest to answer honestly. There isn’t a single typical salary for a high-ticket closer, because pay depends on the company, the offer, the leads and the agreement you sign. What we can do is explain how the pay is put together, so you can understand any offer you’re given.

## Commission-based pay

Most closing roles include commission: a percentage of the value of each sale you close. If you close a sale and your agreed commission is a certain percentage, you earn that share of the amount the customer pays.

The percentage is set by the company and varies between businesses and offers. Some agreements also pay a different rate depending on whether the customer pays in full or in instalments.

## Fixed salary vs commission

Roles generally fall into one of three types:

* **Commission only.** You’re paid only when you close sales. There’s more potential variation, and no income in a month with no sales.
* **Base plus commission.** A fixed salary or fee, with commission on top. Usually more predictable, sometimes with a lower commission rate.
* **Fixed pay.** Less common in high-ticket closing, but some employers offer a set salary with bonuses.

For beginners, the predictability of a base can be valuable while you’re still building your skills. Always check whether you’re being offered employment or a self-employed contractor arrangement, as this affects tax, rights and responsibilities.

## Payment timing

When you’re paid matters as much as how much. Agreements might pay commission weekly, fortnightly or monthly, and some only pay once the customer’s payment has cleared or a refund period has passed.

If a customer pays in instalments, you may receive your commission in instalments too, only as each payment is collected. That can mean waiting months to receive the full amount from one sale.

## Lead quality

A closer’s results depend heavily on who they’re speaking to. Leads who have applied, been qualified and genuinely want help are more likely to buy than leads who booked on impulse. The same closer can see very different results on different offers simply because of lead quality. When assessing a role, it’s reasonable to ask how leads are generated and qualified.

## Show rates

Not everyone who books a call turns up. The show rate is the share of booked calls that actually take place. If you’re commission only, a call that doesn’t happen pays nothing, however many you have in the diary. Show rates vary by company and by how well calls are confirmed and reminded.

## Close rates

The close rate is the share of calls that end in a sale. It’s influenced by your skill, but also by lead quality, the offer, the price and how well the offer fits the people booking. Be wary of anyone who quotes a close rate as if it were guaranteed.

## Refunds and clawbacks

If a customer buys and then receives a refund, many agreements take back, or “claw back”, the commission you were paid on that sale. This might be deducted from your next payment. Read this section of any agreement carefully: how long the clawback period lasts and how it’s applied can make a real difference to your income.

## Realistic earning variability

Closers’ incomes can vary a great deal between people and between months. A new closer is still learning, may be given fewer or lower-quality calls at first and will have quieter weeks. Experienced closers on strong offers can earn well, but that usually reflects time, skill and the right company.

If anyone promises you a specific income, treat it as a warning sign.
