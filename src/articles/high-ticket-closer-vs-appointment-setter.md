In many high-ticket sales teams, the work is split between two roles: appointment setters and closers. They work closely together, but the day-to-day jobs are quite different. Understanding both will help you choose where to start.

## The short version

**An appointment setter** starts conversations with interested leads, checks whether they’re a good fit and books qualified people onto a sales call.

**A closer** runs that sales call, helps the prospect decide whether the offer is right for them and, if it is, completes the sale.

## Responsibilities

**Appointment setters usually:**

* Respond to enquiries, form submissions or social media messages
* Ask qualifying questions to check whether someone is a good fit
* Book suitable leads into the closer’s calendar
* Send reminders to help people show up to their calls
* Keep accurate notes so the closer is well prepared

**Closers usually:**

* Prepare for each call by reviewing the lead’s information
* Run the full sales conversation, from rapport to decision
* Discuss the investment and payment options
* Handle questions and concerns honestly
* Follow up with people who need more time
* Record outcomes and notes in the CRM

## Daily activities

A setter’s day tends to involve a high volume of shorter interactions: messages, quick calls and booking appointments. The work is often spread throughout the day as new enquiries arrive.

A closer’s day is built around fewer, longer, scheduled calls, which can each last up to an hour or more, with preparation and follow-up between them. Closers may also join team training and call reviews.

## Commission structures

Both roles are often commission-based, but the details vary widely between companies.

* **Setters** may be paid per qualified call booked, per call that actually takes place, a percentage of sales from calls they booked, a base rate, or a mix.
* **Closers** are commonly paid a percentage of the sales they close, sometimes alongside a base salary or fixed fee, and sometimes commission only.

Because closers handle the final sale, their commission per sale is typically higher than a setter’s. Every agreement is different, though, so always read the terms carefully. Our guide to [closer earnings](/high-ticket-closer-salary/) covers payment timing, refunds and clawbacks.

## Skills

Both roles need good communication and organisation, but the emphasis differs.

**Setters** benefit from being quick, friendly and consistent, with good written communication and the discipline to follow up with many people.

**Closers** need depth: strong discovery questions, active listening, the ability to connect someone’s goals to an offer, confidence discussing money and calm, ethical handling of concerns.

## Beginner suitability

Appointment setting is often seen as a gentler entry point, because conversations are shorter and the stakes of each one are lower. It’s also a useful way to learn how a sales team works and to hear what prospects care about.

That said, some people with strong communication backgrounds move straight into closing roles that are open to beginners. The right choice depends on your confidence, your experience and how quickly you need a steadier income.

## Career progression

A common path is to start as a setter, learn the process, then progress to closing. Experienced closers may later move into senior closer roles, sales management, training other closers or consulting on sales processes.

None of these steps is automatic. Progress usually comes from consistent results, good feedback and continued practice.

## Which should you choose?

Ask yourself:

* Would I prefer many short conversations or fewer long ones?
* How confident do I feel discussing prices and decisions?
* How many hours can I commit, and when?
* Do I need some predictable income while I learn?
