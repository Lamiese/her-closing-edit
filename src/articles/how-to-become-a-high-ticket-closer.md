Plenty of people start in high-ticket closing without a sales background. What they usually have instead is a set of communication skills, a willingness to practise and a sensible plan. This guide walks you through that plan, step by step.

## Start with your transferable skills

You may already have more relevant experience than you realise. Closing draws on skills used in many other jobs:

* **Listening and questioning**, from roles in customer service, hospitality, healthcare, coaching or teaching
* **Building rapport quickly**, from working with clients or the public
* **Staying calm under pressure**, from busy, demanding or emotional environments
* **Explaining things clearly**, from training others or presenting
* **Handling “no”**, from any job involving negotiation, targets or difficult conversations
* **Being organised**, from managing diaries, admin or your own business

Write down specific examples of when you’ve used each one. You’ll need them later for your CV, your Loom video and your interviews.

## Learn the foundations

Before you apply anywhere, make sure you understand the basics:

* What a closer does and how a sales call is structured (start with [what is high-ticket closing?](/what-is-high-ticket-closing/))
* The difference between closing and [appointment setting](/high-ticket-closer-vs-appointment-setter/)
* How commission works and what affects it (see [how much high-ticket closers earn](/high-ticket-closer-salary/))
* The core skills: discovery questions, active listening, presenting value, discussing price and handling concerns honestly

There’s a lot of free material available, including books on consultative selling, podcasts and videos from experienced sales professionals. Use it to build a good grounding before you consider paying for anything.

## Practise sales conversations

Reading about sales only takes you so far. Closing is a spoken skill, so you need to practise out loud.

* **Role-play with a friend.** Ask them to play a prospect with a realistic situation and a few concerns. Swap roles, too, so you feel what it’s like on the other side.
* **Record yourself.** Listen back for how much you talk compared with how much you listen, whether your questions are open, and how you sound when you mention a price.
* **Practise saying the price.** Many beginners rush or apologise when they state an investment. Practise saying a number calmly and then pausing.
* **Write your own discovery questions.** Then practise asking follow-up questions that go a layer deeper.

Small, regular practice sessions tend to be more useful than one long one.

## Understand the different roles

Not every role suits every beginner. Some people start as appointment setters, where the work is shorter conversations and qualifying leads, before moving into closing. Others go straight for closing roles that accept beginners. Some companies hire for a combined setter and closer role.

Think honestly about which suits you, how many hours you can offer and whether you need some fixed income while you learn. Our [closer vs appointment setter guide](/high-ticket-closer-vs-appointment-setter/) compares the two.

## Build a beginner CV

Your CV should make it easy for a sales manager to see why you’d be good on the phone. Keep it to one or two pages and focus on:

* A short summary explaining that you’re moving into high-ticket sales and why
* Relevant experience, written around outcomes and conversations rather than tasks
* Your transferable skills, each with a real example
* Any sales training you’ve completed, described accurately
* Your availability and time zone

Never exaggerate your experience or invent results. Sales managers will test your skills in interview, and honesty is a large part of what they’re hiring for.

## Record a Loom application

Many remote sales roles ask for a short video introduction, often recorded with a tool called Loom. This lets the company see how you come across before they book an interview.

A good beginner video is usually short and structured:

1. Who you are and where you’re based
2. Why you’re interested in closing, and in this company specifically
3. One or two examples of relevant skills or experience
4. Your availability
5. A friendly close, thanking them for watching

Record in a quiet space with good light and a tidy background. Look at the camera, speak at a calm pace and follow any specific instructions in the job post. Ignoring instructions is one of the quickest ways to be filtered out.

## Evaluate training options carefully

You don’t have to buy a course to become a closer, but structured training and coaching can help some people learn faster. If you’re considering paying for training, ask:

* Who teaches it, and what is their real, verifiable experience?
* What exactly is included: live coaching, call reviews, role-play practice?
* Does it promise a job or a particular income? (If so, be cautious.)
* What is the refund policy?
* Can you speak to past students independently?

Good training should be clear about what it offers and what it doesn’t.

## Avoid unrealistic income promises

You’ll see big numbers online. Some are real, many are exceptional and some are simply untrue. Income from closing depends on the company, the offer, the lead quality, your skill and the agreement you sign, and it can vary considerably from month to month.

It’s wise to have a financial cushion or other income while you learn, and to treat any guarantee of earnings as a warning sign rather than a selling point.
